## Is Ricotta Cheese Safe for Dogs?

Plain ricotta isn't toxic to dogs — and it's actually **one of the gentler members of the dairy family.** It's a fresh whey cheese that's **low in lactose** and **lower in fat than most cheese**, so a small spoonful of plain ricotta is a low-concern treat for most dogs.

This page stays a **Caution** rather than reaching Safe not because of the ricotta itself, but because of **what usually comes with it.** Three things:

- **"Ricotta salata"** is a completely different, salt-cured cheese that's high in sodium.
- **Sweet ricotta** (cannoli, cheesecake, ricotta with honey) is really a sugar question — and cannoli has a chocolate catch.
- **Ricotta is rarely eaten plain** — it's usually baked into lasagna, stuffed shells, or manicotti, where the fat, salt, and garlic or onion in the *dish* matter more than the ricotta.

So: plain fresh ricotta in moderation is fine; the dish it's in is the thing to actually read. (For cheese in general, see the [cheese](/cheese/) page.)

## Where Ricotta Sits Among the Dairy Options

We've calibrated the whole dairy cluster, so here's where ricotta lands on the two axes that matter for dogs:

- **Lactose — low.** Ricotta is a fresh whey cheese with roughly **2 g of lactose per quarter-cup** — well below [milk](/milk/) (~12 g/cup) and regular [yogurt](/yogurt/), and roughly level with [sour cream](/sour-cream/) and cottage cheese. Lactose is a small issue for most dogs here.
- **Fat — moderate, and gentler than most cheese.** Whole-milk ricotta is about **10–12% fat**, part-skim about **5–7%** — *lower* than [sour cream](/sour-cream/) (18–20%) and most aged cheeses, because it's whey-based.

So ricotta is **gentler than milk and sour cream** on both counts. Dairy tolerance is still individual — some dogs handle a dollop fine, others get loose stools — but ricotta is a soft place to start if you're going to share any dairy.

## Fat

Fat is the main everyday caveat for the plain cheese. A **spoonful is minor**, but a **large serving is a real fat load** — and a high-fat meal is a genuine **pancreatitis** trigger, particularly in small dogs and breeds prone to it. Part-skim ricotta roughly halves the fat if that's a concern. The [bacon](/bacon/) page has the full detail on how fat drives pancreatitis and which dogs are most at risk.

## Salt — and Why "Ricotta Salata" Is a Different Story

This is the ricotta detail most people don't know, and it matters: **"ricotta" and "ricotta salata" are not the same cheese.**

- **Plain fresh ricotta** (the soft, spoonable kind) is **low in sodium** — fine on the salt front.
- **Ricotta salata** is the **aged, salt-rubbed, firm-and-crumbly** version — grated over pasta and salads, used more like feta. The salt-curing process makes it **high in sodium: roughly 400 mg per ounce** (around 916 mg per cup).

So don't conflate the two. A spoon of fresh ricotta is a low-salt treat; ricotta salata is a genuinely salty cheese, and worth keeping away from small dogs or any dog on a vet-prescribed low-sodium diet. See the [salt](/salt/) page for sodium context.

## The Dishes Ricotta Comes In

Here's the honest part: ricotta is **rarely eaten plain.** It's baked into **lasagna, stuffed shells, manicotti, baked ziti, and ravioli** — and in those dishes, **the ricotta is the mild ingredient.** The real considerations come from the dish around it: extra fat (cheese blends and oil), salt, and — most importantly — the **garlic and onion that are almost always in the tomato or marinara sauce.**

It's worth being precise about this, because it's different from a food like sour-cream-and-onion dip, where the allium is mixed *into the product itself.* With ricotta, the allium is usually in the **sauce**, not the cheese — so a dog eating a serving of lasagna is getting fat, salt, and allium **from the dish**, not from the ricotta. The one place it becomes direct is a **garlic-herb ricotta filling**, where garlic is mixed straight into the cheese.

Either way, the practical rule is the same: **if a ricotta dish contains garlic or onion — in the sauce or the filling — treat that as an [allium](/onions/) exposure** ([garlic](/garlic/) and onion are genuinely toxic to dogs) and judge it by the amount. Plain herbs like [basil](/basil/) and parsley in a herbed ricotta are fine; garlic is the one to check.

## Sweet Ricotta: a Sugar Question

Ricotta also shows up on the dessert side — **cannoli filling, ricotta cheesecake, and ricotta with honey or sugar.** These are really a **sugar** question: empty calories and possible GI upset, and more of a concern for diabetic or overweight dogs (see the [sugar](/sugar/) page).

One specific flag: **cannoli filling almost always contains chocolate chips** (and sometimes candied fruit), so a dog into cannoli is a **sugar *and* [chocolate](/chocolate/)** question — the chocolate being the more serious of the two. Sweet ricotta is a treat-for-humans, not a health snack for dogs.

## Symptoms to Watch For

**From plain ricotta (lactose or fat):**
- Loose stools or diarrhoea, gas, vomiting, abdominal discomfort
- After a large, fatty amount, watch for **pancreatitis** signs over 24–72 hours — repeated vomiting, a hunched/painful posture, marked lethargy, loss of appetite

**From a dish that contained garlic or onion (allium — often delayed 3–5 days):**
- Lethargy, weakness, pale gums, reddish/brown urine, reduced appetite

**From cannoli or chocolate-containing sweet ricotta:**
- Restlessness, a racing heart, vomiting — the [chocolate](/chocolate/) signs

## What To Do

- **A small spoonful of plain fresh ricotta, healthy dog:** low concern. Watch for loose stools if your dog is lactose-sensitive; keep it occasional.
- **A large amount or a whole tub of plain ricotta:** monitor for GI upset and, given the fat, pancreatitis signs over 1–3 days — especially for a small dog or a breed at risk.
- **Ricotta salata, or a salty amount:** mind the sodium, particularly for small dogs or those on a low-sodium diet.
- **A serving of lasagna, stuffed shells, or similar:** the fat, salt, and any garlic/onion in the dish are the concern. Check the recipe; if it was garlic- or onion-heavy, treat it as an [allium](/onions/) exposure and watch over the next 3–5 days.
- **Cannoli or sweet ricotta with chocolate:** check for chocolate, and call your vet or a poison line if a meaningful amount was eaten (see [chocolate](/chocolate/)).

## Frequently Asked Questions

> This FAQ summarises general veterinary and nutrition guidance. It's informational only and not a substitute for advice from your own vet, who knows your dog.

### Can dogs eat lasagna?

Best avoided — but not because of the ricotta. The ricotta layer is the mild part; the concern with lasagna is the **rich fat load** (multiple cheeses and oil), the **salt**, and — most importantly — the **garlic and onion** that are almost always in the tomato sauce, which are genuinely toxic to dogs. A small amount of plain-cheese lasagna is unlikely to harm a healthy dog, but a garlic-and-onion-heavy serving is an [allium](/onions/) exposure worth watching for a few days. Plain ricotta on its own is far gentler than the lasagna it's baked into.

### Is plain ricotta safe for dogs?

In small amounts, yes for most dogs — it's one of the gentler dairy options, low in lactose and moderate in fat. A spoonful of plain, unsalted fresh ricotta is a fine occasional treat; watch for loose stools if your dog is lactose-sensitive, and don't make it a habit.

### Is ricotta better than other cheese for dogs?

On the two things that matter for dogs — lactose and fat — plain fresh ricotta is gentler than most cheeses: low in lactose and, being whey-based, lower in fat than sour cream or aged hard cheeses. The exception is **ricotta salata**, the salt-cured version, which is high in sodium. See the [cheese](/cheese/) page for cheese in general.

### My dog ate ricotta — should I worry?

For a small amount of **plain fresh** ricotta, no — just watch for mild stomach upset. The real questions are **how much** (fat), whether it was the salty **ricotta salata**, and — most often — **what dish it was in** (garlic or onion in the sauce, chocolate in cannoli).
