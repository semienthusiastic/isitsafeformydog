## Is Feta Cheese Safe for Dogs?

Feta isn't toxic to dogs — but of all the dairy we've calibrated, it sits at the **salty end of the family.** If [buttermilk](/buttermilk/) is the gentle pole of the dairy cluster (low fat, low lactose), feta is close to the opposite one: it's a **brined cheese**, sold and stored in salt, and sodium is the real story here.

One thing to correct up front, because it's a common claim and it was on the earlier version of this very page: feta is often described as "high in lactose," and that's not right. Feta is actually **fairly low in lactose** — the brining and aging see to that — so the milk-sugar isn't the concern it is with [milk](/milk/) or [yogurt](/yogurt/). The honest order of concern for feta is **salt first, fat second, and lactose a distant third.** Let's take them in that order, then cover the two allium catches that genuinely apply.

## Salt: the Lead Concern — and Feta Is "Inherently Salty"

Feta carries roughly **312 mg of sodium per ounce.** To put that in cluster terms, that's about **three times as salty as milk** — and it lands right next to **ricotta salata** (~400 mg/oz), the salt-cured cheese we flagged on the [ricotta](/ricotta-cheese/) page. Feta is genuinely one of the saltiest things in the dairy aisle.

Here's the framing that ties feta to a pattern the site keeps meeting: feta is **inherently salty.** There's no "low-salt plain feta" to reach for, because the **brine is what makes feta feta** — take the salt away and it isn't the same cheese. That puts it in a small, recognisable group we've now named across several pages: the **inherently-salty foods**, where the salt isn't an add-on you can leave out but a defining part of the product. Its cluster-mates are **ricotta salata** (the dairy sibling) and **[capers](/capers/)** (the non-dairy one — a brined bud that's salty for the same structural reason). It's the same **separable-vs-inherent** distinction that keeps these foods at Caution: you can core an apple, but you can't de-salt a feta.

Now the calibration, because "saltiest in the aisle" still needs a sense of scale. Veterinary toxicology (Merck) puts genuine **salt toxicosis** in the range of **2–3 grams of salt per kilogram of body weight** — a threshold a dog won't reach from a crumble of feta on a dropped forkful. What feta's saltiness realistically means is more everyday: a **meaningful serving is a real sodium load**, so it's worth **increased thirst, GI upset, and portion-awareness**, especially for **small dogs** and any dog on a **vet-prescribed low-sodium diet**. Feta is eaten in bigger pieces than a caper — cubed into salads, crumbled by the handful — so the "used in tiny amounts" reassurance that softened the caper page applies *less* here. See the [salt](/salt/) page for the full sodium picture.

## Fat: the Secondary Concern

After salt comes fat. Whole-milk feta is about **21% fat** — moderate-to-high, in the neighbourhood of [sour cream](/sour-cream/) and above fresh [ricotta](/ricotta-cheese/) (5–12%). A small crumble is minor, but a **large, fatty serving** carries the usual dairy-fat caveat: GI upset, and — for small or predisposed dogs — a **pancreatitis** risk at genuinely rich quantities. It's a secondary note here, not the headline, but it stacks on top of the salt rather than replacing it. (For cheese in general, see the [cheese](/cheese/) page.)

## The Two Allium Catches — Both Genuine

Feta comes with two garlic-and-onion angles that are *not* a stretch, so they get real weight rather than a passing mention.

### 1. Garlic-herb feta — allium mixed into the cheese itself

**Garlic-herb feta is a common, widely-sold commercial product** — **Athenos Garlic & Herb Feta**, for one, is stocked across major grocery chains. This matters because the garlic is **blended directly into the cheese**, not sitting in a sauce nearby. That puts it in the strongest allium tier on the site — the same **direct-in-product** category as [sour cream](/sour-cream/)-and-onion dip, not the weaker sauce-adjacent case. [Garlic](/garlic/) (and [garlic powder](/garlic-powder/), which is more concentrated than the fresh clove) is genuinely toxic to dogs, damaging red blood cells in a dose-dependent, often delayed way. So a dog eating a chunk of **garlic-herb** feta is having a real [allium](/onions/) exposure on top of the salt — check the label, because "feta" on its own and "garlic-herb feta" are different risk questions.

### 2. Greek salad — the red onion is a standard ingredient

The dish feta turns up in most is **Greek salad**, and here the catch is the **red onion** — which is not an optional garnish but a **traditional, standard ingredient** of the salad, sliced right in alongside the feta, cucumber, tomato, and olives. That makes it a **direct-in-dish** onion exposure, the same tier as the onion in coleslaw — not a trace in a dressing. [Onion](/onions/) is toxic to dogs by the same mechanism as garlic. So a dog that hoovers up a bowl of Greek salad is getting **onion plus a very salty cheese** — judge it by the amount of onion, and watch over the following days if it was a meaningful quantity.

## Spanakopita and Other Feta Dishes

Beyond the salad, feta bakes into **spanakopita** (the Greek spinach-and-feta pastry): layers of **phyllo, [butter](/butter/), [spinach](/spinach/), feta, and usually onion.** As with other baked dishes on the site, the feta is one salty-fatty component among several — the concerns are the **butter-rich fat**, the **salt**, and any **onion** in the filling, rather than the feta alone. A small bite of plain spanakopita is unlikely to harm a healthy dog, but an onion-heavy filling makes it an [allium](/onions/) question. The rule that keeps recurring: when feta is baked into something, read the dish by its other ingredients.

## One Nice Exception: Watermelon and Feta

Worth ending the food-combos on a calmer note. **Watermelon-feta salad** is a popular summer dish, and here the two halves land very differently for a dog: [watermelon](/watermelon/) is genuinely **dog-safe** (seedless, rind removed), a hydrating low-calorie fruit — while the feta is the salty part. So if your dog steals from a watermelon-feta bowl, the **fruit is fine and the cheese is the only thing to think about** — a reassuring inversion of the usual "the dish hides a problem" pattern. The watermelon's the treat; the feta's just salt.

## Symptoms to Watch For

**From the salt or fat (a meaningful serving of plain feta):**
- Increased thirst and urination, vomiting, diarrhoea, gas, abdominal discomfort
- After a large, fatty amount, watch for **pancreatitis** signs over 24–72 hours — repeated vomiting, a hunched or painful posture, marked lethargy, loss of appetite
- After a genuinely large *salty* amount in a small dog, the more serious sodium signs — marked lethargy, wobbliness or tremors — warrant a prompt vet call

**From garlic-herb feta or a Greek salad's onion (allium — often delayed 3–5 days):**
- Lethargy, weakness, pale gums, reddish or brown urine, reduced appetite

## What To Do

- **A small crumble of plain feta, healthy dog:** low concern. Mind the salt; keep it occasional and small; make sure fresh water's available.
- **A large amount of plain feta:** monitor for GI upset and thirst; for a small dog, be mindful of the sodium, and watch for pancreatitis signs over 1–3 days given the fat.
- **Garlic-herb (or other flavoured) feta:** treat a meaningful amount as an [allium](/garlic/) exposure — check the label, and watch over the next 3–5 days.
- **A Greek salad:** the **red onion** is the thing to weigh, not the feta — treat a meaningful amount as an [allium](/onions/) exposure.
- **A low-sodium diet:** feta is one of the saltier dairy choices — skip it and pick something gentler like a little plain [ricotta](/ricotta-cheese/) or plain [buttermilk](/buttermilk/).

## Frequently Asked Questions

> This FAQ summarises general veterinary and nutrition guidance. It's informational only and not a substitute for advice from your own vet, who knows your dog.

### Is feta cheese safe for dogs?

In a small amount of plain feta, it won't poison a healthy dog — but it's a salty, fatty treat rather than a good one. Feta is a brined cheese at around 312 mg of sodium an ounce, roughly three times as salty as milk, so it's best kept to the occasional small crumble, and skipped for small dogs or those on a low-sodium diet. Watch out for **garlic-herb feta** and feta in a **Greek salad**, where garlic or onion is the bigger concern.

### Isn't feta high in lactose?

Not really — that's a common misconception (it was even on the earlier version of this page). Feta's brining and aging leave it **fairly low in lactose**, lower than milk or yogurt, so the milk-sugar isn't the main issue. The real concern with feta is its **salt**, followed by its fat. Lactose-sensitive dogs may still get loose stools from any dairy, but feta isn't a high-lactose food.

### My dog ate garlic-herb feta — should I worry?

Take it more seriously than plain feta. Garlic-herb feta has **garlic blended right into the cheese**, and garlic is genuinely toxic to dogs — it damages red blood cells in a dose-dependent, often delayed way. A tiny taste is unlikely to cause harm, but a meaningful amount is a real [allium](/garlic/) exposure: watch for lethargy, weakness, pale gums, or reddish urine over the next 3–5 days, and call your vet if the amount was significant or symptoms appear.

### Can dogs eat Greek salad?

Best not — because of the **red onion**, not the feta. Onion is a standard ingredient in Greek salad and is toxic to dogs, so a bowl of it is an [allium](/onions/) exposure on top of a very salty cheese. A stray cucumber or tomato is harmless, but don't share the salad as a whole; if your dog ate a meaningful amount, watch for the onion signs over the following days.
