## Are Apples Safe for Dogs?

**Yes — and unlike a lot of "yes, but…" foods, apples are genuinely good for dogs.** They're a low-calorie, high-fibre treat with vitamins A and C, and both the **flesh and the skin** are fine. The American Kennel Club actively recommends them as a healthy snack.

There are really only two things to get right, and neither is a reason for alarm:

- **Remove the core** before you give one over — the tough, woody core is a **choking hazard** (this is the real reason to core an apple, more than the seeds).
- **Keep portions sensible** — a slice or two — because apples do carry real sugar.

We've marked this page **Safe** (you'll see apples listed with cautions all over the internet, largely because of the seeds — and we previously erred that way too). On the actual numbers, a cored apple is a safe, healthy treat: the seed "cyanide" worry doesn't survive contact with the dose math, and the core is a preparation step, not a property of the fruit. Here's the honest calibration.

## Apple Seeds and Cyanide: Why It's a Non-Issue in Practice

The scary version is technically true and practically almost meaningless. Apple seeds contain **amygdalin**, which can release **cyanide** — but for a dog, the realistic risk is essentially nil, for three independent reasons:

1. **The seeds have to be crushed to release any cyanide.** The Merck Veterinary Manual is explicit that "crushing and mastication" are what release cyanogenic compounds from plant cells. **Apple seeds swallowed whole pass straight through** and release next to nothing — and dogs, gulping rather than grinding, swallow most seeds whole.
2. **The amount per seed is tiny** — roughly 0.01 mg of cyanide per crushed seed.
3. **The toxic dose is enormous.** Merck puts the acute lethal cyanide dose at about **2 mg/kg**. For a **10 kg (22 lb) dog** that's ~20 mg — around **2,000 thoroughly-crushed seeds**; for a **5 kg dog**, ~1,000. An apple holds 5–10 seeds, so that's the crushed seeds of **roughly 100–200 apples.** It simply doesn't happen by accident.

So a dog that snatched a tossed apple core with a handful of seeds is **not in danger.** Coring apples is still sensible practice — it removes the seeds *and* the choking hazard in one move — but do it as tidy habit, not out of fear. (This is genuinely different from [grapes](/grapes/), which **are** toxic to dogs at realistic amounts — don't lump the two together just because both are fruit.)

## The Core Itself: the Real Hazard to Take Seriously

Set the cyanide myth aside and there's still one apple part that deserves respect — but it's a **mechanical** hazard, not a chemical one. The **tough, woody core and the stem can be a choking or gastrointestinal-obstruction risk**, especially for **small dogs** or enthusiastic gulpers who swallow chunks without chewing.

This is the actual reason to core an apple before sharing it:

- Cut the apple into **slices or bite-sized pieces**, and cut out the **core and stem**.
- Size the pieces to your dog — smaller for small dogs.
- Supervise, as you would with any chew.

A dog that swallowed a core won't be poisoned, but it's worth watching for signs of obstruction (below) — this is the one apple scenario that can genuinely need a vet.

## Why Apple Flesh Is Actually Good for Dogs

This isn't a "harmless but pointless" treat — apple has real merits. Per the AKC, apples are:

- **High in fibre** — especially in the skin — which supports digestion and helps with healthy weight.
- **A good source of vitamins A and C**, plus potassium and antioxidants.
- **Low in calories**, which makes apple slices a genuinely useful treat for weight-conscious or food-motivated dogs.

The **skin is fine** — in fact it holds most of the fibre — so there's no need to peel; just wash it. A few apple slices make an excellent low-guilt reward.

## Sugar and Portion

The one nutritional caveat is **sugar**: a whole apple has roughly **19 g of sugar**, so apples are a *treat*, not a staple. A **slice or two** is plenty; too much can cause a bellyache or loose stools, and the sugar matters more for **diabetic or overweight dogs**, where it's worth checking with your vet. See the [sugar](/sugar/) page for the broader picture. (The same "great in moderation, mind the sugar" logic applies to [pears](/pears/) and most fruit.)

## Apple Products — What Changes

- **Applesauce:** plain, **unsweetened** applesauce is fine in moderation and much like apple flesh. Many commercial versions add sugar, so **choose unsweetened.**
- **Dried apple / apple chips:** fine in small amounts, but drying concentrates the sugar, so treat them as a more sugary version.
- **Sugar-free / "no sugar added" apple products and [xylitol](/xylitol/):** a quick label check is worth it on any sugar-free food — but unlike some sugar-free syrups (where xylitol genuinely shows up, see [maple syrup](/maple-syrup/)), **mainstream "no sugar added" applesauce is simply unsweetened apple with no added sweetener.** Xylitol isn't a typical apple-product ingredient; read the label on anything sugar-free, but this is a low concern here.
- **Cinnamon:** cinnamon applesauce and apple-pie spice are fine — [cinnamon](/cinnamon/) is non-toxic to dogs in the small amounts involved. **Apple pie filling**, though, is mostly sugar (and sometimes other spices), so that's a sugar question, not a health treat.

## Symptoms to Watch For

Apple is not a poisoning risk, so there's not much to watch for — but two mild scenarios:

**From too much apple (the sugar/fibre):**
- Loose stools or diarrhoea, gas, mild stomach upset

**From a swallowed core (the one to actually watch — possible obstruction, especially in small dogs):**
- Gagging, retching, or drooling right after
- Repeated vomiting, or bringing food back up
- Loss of appetite, lethargy, or straining/failing to pass stool over the following day or two

## What To Do

- **A cored apple slice or two, healthy dog:** enjoy — this is a good treat, no action needed.
- **Ate a lot of apple:** monitor for a bellyache or loose stools; keep water available. Cut back the portion next time.
- **Ate the core or stem:** don't worry about poisoning, but watch for the obstruction signs above — especially in a small dog — and call your vet if any appear or your dog seems unwell.
- **Ate the seeds:** a few seeds are not a cyanide concern; there's nothing to do. (It would take the crushed seeds of many apples to matter.)
- **Ate a sugar-free apple product:** check the label for xylitol; if it's listed (uncommon in apple products) and a meaningful amount was eaten, call your vet.

## Frequently Asked Questions

> This FAQ summarises general veterinary and nutrition guidance. It's informational only and not a substitute for advice from your own vet, who knows your dog.

### Are apple seeds poisonous to dogs?

In theory yes, in practice no. Apple seeds contain amygdalin, which can release cyanide — but the seeds must be **crushed** to release any (swallowed whole, they pass straight through), the amount is tiny (~0.01 mg per seed), and the toxic dose is huge: a dog would need to chew up the seeds of **roughly a hundred or more apples** to be at risk. A few seeds from a tossed core won't hurt your dog. It's still sensible to core apples — mostly because of the choking hazard of the core — but incidental seeds are not an emergency.

### Can dogs eat apple skin?

Yes. The skin is fine and actually one of the healthiest parts — most of the fibre is in the peel. Just wash it before serving.

### Can dogs eat applesauce?

Plain, unsweetened applesauce, yes, in moderation. Choose **unsweetened** — sweetened applesauce adds sugar — and give a quick glance at the label of any "sugar-free" version for xylitol (uncommon in applesauce, but worth the check).

### How much apple can a dog have?

A slice or two is plenty. A whole apple has about 19 g of sugar, so keep it an occasional treat rather than a daily habit — and be more careful with diabetic or overweight dogs. Too much apple mostly causes a bellyache, not anything dangerous.

### My dog ate a whole apple, core and all — should I worry?

Not about poisoning — the flesh and seeds aren't a toxic concern. The thing to watch is whether the **core** caused a problem: gagging, retching, repeated vomiting, or trouble passing stool, especially in a small dog. If any of that shows up, call your vet. Otherwise a bit of loose stool from the sugar is the likely worst of it.
